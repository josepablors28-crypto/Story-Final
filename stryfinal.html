a family story, told secondhand

Whoever Held the Phone

There was one telephone for the whole house — and whoever answered it decided which messages made it through, and which drifted into nothing. Tegucigalpa, Honduras. Late 1980s.

Chapter One

The Only Phone in the House

Back in those days, there were only one or two designated phones for an entire household in Honduras — which meant anyone in the house could pick it up, and meant it was hard for two people to ever speak privately. Whoever had the phone in hand had the power to let a message through, or let it slip into the abyss of nothingness.

My mother was the third of four children, and by the time she reached high school she had little say over the household's only line. Her older brother Gerardo — the second oldest, a senior, and by most accounts the most popular kid in school — was glued to it constantly. He was a DJ with a wide collection of cassettes, a magnet for calls from every direction, and entirely unwilling to hand the receiver to his younger sister.

On the rare afternoons she got home before him, my mother would sneak in a call to a friend. Then Gerardo would arrive, and she would retreat to her room in resignation, bracing for his wrath. It went on like this for months, until she began to half-believe that maybe no one really wanted to talk to her at all.

Chapter Two

The Parties

Gerardo's cassette collection made him something of a legend, and he threw some of the best high school parties of his generation — usually in their own house, with kids from every grade filing through. My mother was allowed to attend, and every so often a boy would come talk to her and make her smile. But Gerardo, playing the jealous older brother, would appear almost instantly to pull her away.

the disco ball over Gerardo's living-room parties

Chapter Three

The Flowers

"Hey Erika, I've been trying to call you but you don't seem to respond, so I figured it would be a good idea to stop by and give you these flowers — maybe we can go grab dinner sometime."

Then, one Saturday morning, came the flowers. My mother later said that if she hadn't woken early that day, Gerardo would likely have just thrown them away. But he was still asleep after a long night, and she was the one who went down to answer the knock at the door.

Standing there was a boy she'd spoken to only briefly at a party — the one she'd thought was very handsome. He played on the soccer team, was his class's valedictorian, and was holding a jumble of flowers.

She smiled and simply nodded, butterflies rising as if Cupid himself had found his mark. That boy was my father. Gerardo later admitted the truth — he'd been fielding calls from boy after boy asking for her, and hanging up on all of them, telling each one they had the wrong number. My father, it turned out, had been captivated enough to skip the phone altogether and just knock on the door.

Chapter Four

The Stares

As the parties went on, my mother had begun noticing something strange — angry, unreadable looks from boys she hadn't even met, in hallways, at parties, from faces she couldn't place. She had no idea what was going on, only that something was happening just out of her sight. It was only later that she understood why.

Twenty-five years later

They still laugh about it

The marriage has lasted more than twenty-five years, and when my parents bring it up with Uncle Gerardo, the three of them still laugh in unison — grateful, every time, that he happened to sleep in. If it hadn't been for that one overslept morning, I wouldn't be here writing this story.
